The intersection that waits for you to finish your thought.

At two in the morning a square stops being a diagram and becomes a room. Someone is still holding the levers.

The first thing you notice is not the noise. It is the choreography: a bus that is allowed four extra seconds, a cyclist who takes none, an operator who lifts one finger and changes the mood of the square.1 Automation promises consistency. The night prefers negotiation.

Ana has run the same console for eleven years. It is a metal box with eight levers and a fan that sounds like it is thinking. She never says “flow”. She says she lets people finish their thought, and then she counts to four.2

A city is not a machine that runs. It is a conversation that keeps going.

By four the square has changed hands three times. The delivery vans leave, the cleaners arrive, the first runners appear like a rumour. The operator stays and the lights keep her rhythm. None of it is automatic, and none of it is in the plan.3
